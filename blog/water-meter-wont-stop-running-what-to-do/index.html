What to Do When Your Water Meter Won't Stop Running

One of the clearest signs of a hidden leak, and one of the most misunderstood.

Written by the Clearwater Express Plumbing Team · September 16, 2026

DISPATCH LINE
(727) 931-8107
Call Now
Intro

This Is A Real Diagnostic Signal

A water meter that keeps turning even when every fixture in the house is off is one of the clearest, most reliable signs of a hidden leak somewhere in your system. Here's how to check it yourself and what the result actually tells you.

How To Check It

The Simple Test Anyone Can Do

Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, including ice makers and irrigation systems. Locate your water meter, usually near the street or at the edge of your property, and note the reading. Wait 20 to 30 minutes without using any water, then check the reading again. Any change means water is flowing somewhere you're not seeing it.

What It Doesn't Tell You

Confirmed Leak, Unknown Location

A moving meter confirms a leak exists, but it doesn't tell you where. The leak could be inside the house, under the slab, or even in the supply line between the meter and the house, all of which require different diagnostic approaches to actually locate.

Common Culprits

Where To Look First

A running toilet is the most common cause of a moving meter and the easiest to rule out, listen closely at each toilet or add food coloring to the tank to see if it appears in the bowl without flushing. If every toilet checks out, the leak is more likely in a supply line, either inside a wall, under the slab, or underground between the meter and the house.

When To Call A Professional

Beyond The Toilet Check

If you've ruled out toilets and the meter is still moving, that's the point to call a professional rather than start guessing at walls or flooring. Acoustic leak detection equipment can locate the actual source without unnecessary digging or demolition.

Why Waiting Costs More

The Bill Isn't The Only Risk

Beyond the water bill itself, an undetected leak, especially a slab or underground leak, can cause structural damage, mold, and soil erosion around a foundation over time. The cost of finding it early is almost always lower than the cost of the damage it eventually causes.

Irrigation Systems

A Commonly Overlooked Source

If your home has an irrigation system, a leak in an underground zone valve or line can also cause a moving meter without any obvious sign indoors. It's worth checking whether your irrigation system is on the same meter as your home's domestic water before assuming the leak is inside the house.

Reading Your Bill

What A Real Comparison Looks Like

Comparing your last few months of billed usage, not just the total dollar amount, gives a clearer picture than the bill total alone, since utility rates can change independently of actual usage. A sudden jump in gallons used, even if the dollar increase looks modest, is worth investigating.

Smart Meters

A Newer Tool Worth Knowing About

Some Pinellas County utilities now offer smart meter data homeowners can check online, sometimes showing hourly usage patterns rather than just a monthly total. If your utility offers this, a spike during hours when the house should be empty is another useful way to confirm a hidden leak without waiting for a full billing cycle.

What If The Leak Is Between The Meter And The House

A Less Common But Real Possibility

Occasionally, the leak is in the service line between the street meter and the house itself, technically outside the home but still the homeowner's responsibility to repair in most cases. This is less common than an indoor leak but is exactly the kind of thing acoustic detection is built to isolate.

Documenting For Your Utility

Why It's Worth Keeping Records

Some Pinellas County utilities offer a one-time high-bill adjustment for a documented, repaired leak, but they typically require proof the leak was found and fixed. Keeping the invoice from whoever locates and repairs the leak is worth doing specifically for this reason, beyond just your own records.

A Quick Recap

The Short Version If You're Skimming

A meter that keeps moving with everything off confirms a leak exists somewhere, though not where. Rule out toilets first, then call for acoustic detection if the meter is still moving, since waiting risks real structural and mold damage beyond just the water bill.

A Final Practical Note

Keep A Record Of Your Test

Writing down the exact meter readings and times from your own test gives a technician a useful starting reference, and it's also useful documentation if you end up requesting a high-bill adjustment from your utility later.

FAQs

Common Questions

Could a moving meter be a false alarm?

It's rare. A truly still meter after 20-30 minutes of no water use is a reliable no-leak indicator, and a moving one is a reliable sign something is running somewhere.

Is checking my own meter safe to do?

Yes, it just involves reading the dial, no tools or access to plumbing required.

Could my irrigation system be causing this instead of my house?

Yes, if it shares a meter with your home. Checking whether your irrigation zones are off during the test is worth doing before assuming the leak is indoors.

Could the leak actually be outside my house, between the meter and my home?

Yes, that's a real possibility and still generally the homeowner's responsibility to repair, which is exactly the kind of leak acoustic detection is built to pinpoint.

How quickly should I act once I confirm the meter is moving?

Promptly, since an undetected leak keeps costing money and risking damage every day it goes unaddressed.

Can a moving meter ever be caused by something other than a leak?

Rarely, but a stuck fixture valve or a running irrigation zone can sometimes mimic a leak, which is part of why ruling out the obvious causes first matters.

Is it worth checking my meter periodically even without a specific reason to suspect a leak?

A quick check every few months is a reasonable habit, since it can catch a slow leak well before it shows up as a major bill spike.

Does the meter test work the same way for a home on well water?

Homes on private wells typically don't have a utility meter, so a different diagnostic approach, usually a pressure tank cycle check, applies instead.

Should I call my utility company or a plumber first if I suspect a leak?

Either is reasonable, but a plumber can actually locate and fix the leak, while the utility mainly confirms usage data.
